ENTRY 01Token Tracker
Nov 19–21, 20266 runs · 6 pass

Cutting AI usage by about 40%, without cutting quality.

While building Exocorder, an Android app I'm developing, my AI usage grew faster than I could sustain. I didn't want to guess at the cause, so I set out to measure it.

40.7%fewer tokens
6 / 6runs passed the test
2 / 3tasks with the most thorough tests, judged blind

What I built

A runner that gives the AI one written task at a time, checks the result with a fixed test, and records exactly how many tokens the task used. "Done" is decided by the test, not by the AI. Every task runs on a throwaway copy of the real project, so nothing touches the original.

My first attempt failed, and I kept the record

My first method saved only 8% against a plain AI session, far short of my 50% goal. The session records showed why: the AI barely used the code map I gave it, and reading code was three-quarters of the cost.

What worked

The biggest lever turned out to be a setting: how hard the model "thinks." Lowering it, filtering what the AI is shown, and keeping its output short brought a lean version to about half the tokens.

Why I gave some of that back

A separate reviewer graded the AI's work without knowing which setup produced it or what it cost. The lean version was writing thinner tests. I added rules that require full coverage and made them the default. That costs more tokens, and it was the right trade. Today's setup ranked most thorough on two of three tasks in the first round.

What this doesn't show. These are tokens, not dollars; I use a subscription. It's a small sample: three real tasks, run twice each, and one task varied a lot between runs. Most of the saving comes from the effort setting; my method adds about 13% on top. AI wrote the code. My part was the problem, the rules, the tests, and deciding what to keep.

ENTRY 03How I work
every project

8 lines of my SOPs.

(standard operating procedures)

  1. Define done first. What the result must be, what it must not break, and what waits for later.
  2. Reuse before building. Compare the tools that already exist, and pick the smallest one that does the job.
  3. Turn the goal into bounded work. A written task with limits, a budget, and a test that decides when it's done.
  4. Direct the AI; own the result. The AI writes the code. I set the context, the rules and the boundaries.
  5. Check it independently. Judge the exact result against the test, with a separate reviewer where quality matters.
  6. Measure the real cost. Count retries, corrections and failed work, not just the cheapest request.
  7. Write it down. Decisions, procedures and handoff notes, so someone else can pick it up without me.
  8. Improve from evidence. Change the method only when the record shows it should change.

The full guide is public: How I Work, the operating documents and templates behind these eight lines.

I give you an honest account, including my mistakes.

When I find an error in my own numbers, I correct it in public and keep the original visible.

The correction

An early result read 58% 42%. Two test runs had shared the AI's memory, which made one look cheaper. I published the correction and changed the runner so runs can't share it.

Where the habits come from.

Iraq · 2004–05

I find out what people actually need before anything gets built.

In Iraq I turned conversations with local leaders into building plans, including the rebuilding of a courthouse in Baghdad.

The story

I was cross-leveled into a civil affairs unit rebuilding the country. I sat down with local leaders and sheikhs through an interpreter, asked what their communities needed, and wrote up what should be built. My superiors decided from those write-ups. One of them became the rebuilding of a courthouse in Baghdad, under my guidance. I was a private first class.

Iraq · 2004–05

If I'm the only one who knows a system, I make sure I'm not.

As the unit's only radio operator, I trained an authorized backup so the radios never depended on me alone.

The story

Every week the line formed at 5 a.m. so I could load encryption into everyone's radios before I headed out, and I briefed the lieutenant colonel of the infantry battalion we were attached to. Because I couldn't always be there, I got a cleared soldier authorized as my backup, set her up with secure storage, and showed her how to run the system without me. I went from private first class to sergeant in a year.

Kuwait · 2010–11

I've been tracking expensive things for a long time.

As a sergeant, I led an eight-person section running the systems that tracked our trucks during the drawdown from Iraq.

The story

Our transportation unit moved equipment out of Iraq. We ran RFID readers at sites across the route, so we knew roughly where every vehicle was. Think UPS, somewhere far more volatile. We kept those systems running and troubleshot the network segments we shared with other units' communications sections. Measuring where expensive things go, and keeping the tracking itself reliable, is what Token Tracker does for AI usage.

Kuwait · 2010–11

Nobody gets more access than the job needs. That includes AI.

I enforced my unit's computer security and issued classified-network access only after clearance. I set AI up the same way.

The story

As my unit's computer security compliance officer, I made sure badges stayed visible and no one walked away with their access card still in the computer. I wrote people up for it. For the classified network, SIPRNet, nobody got a card until the intelligence officer had cleared them, and then I issued it. I also wrote the SOPs that turned those rules into steps soldiers could follow.

An AI tool should get the same treatment: only the access its job needs. In Token Tracker, the AI works on a throwaway copy of the project and is told exactly which files it may change. The only commands it can run are the task's check and a code-map lookup; anything else is refused and logged. A budget cap or an access rule only protects you if it's enforced every time.
